If by **A*** you mean “top grade in your AP Chemistry class and ideally a **5 on the AP exam**,” then we can build a precise mastery checklist. AP itself reports exam scores from **1 to 5**, rather than A*–F grades. Tiny naming inconvenience, because apparently education systems needed several incompatible ways to say “did well.” ([AP 学生](https://apstudents.collegeboard.org/about-ap-scores/score-distributions/ap-chemistry?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Past AP Chemistry Score Distributions"))

The current College Board AP Chemistry framework has **9 units**. Unit 3 is especially large at **18–22%**, Unit 8 is **11–15%**, and the other seven units are each **7–9%** of the multiple-choice content weighting. More importantly, every AP question combines **chemistry content with a science skill**, so simply memorizing definitions will not get you to the top score. ([AP中央](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-course-and-exam-description.pdf "AP® Chemistry Course and Exam Description"))

Below is the master checklist I would use.

# UNIT 1: Atomic Structure and Properties

## Official topics 1.1–1.8

The official topics are moles and molar mass, mass spectra, elemental composition, composition of mixtures, atomic structure/electron configuration, photoelectron spectroscopy, periodic trends, and valence electrons/ionic compounds. ([AP中央](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-course-and-exam-description.pdf "AP® Chemistry Course and Exam Description"))

### 1.1 Moles and molar mass

Know:

- **mole**

- **Avogadro's number**

- particle

- atom

- molecule

- formula unit

- molar mass

- atomic mass unit, amu

- relative atomic mass


Core relationship:

$$1\text{ mol}=6.022\times10^{23}$$
Master conversions:

$$\text{grams}\leftrightarrow\text{moles}$$
$$\text{moles}\leftrightarrow\text{particles}$$
$$\text{grams}\leftrightarrow\text{particles}$$
using:

$$n=\frac{m}{M}$$
You need to understand why molar mass numerically connects microscopic particle mass in amu with macroscopic mass in grams per mole. ([AP中央](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-course-and-exam-description.pdf "AP® Chemistry Course and Exam Description"))

### 1.2 Mass spectra of elements

Know:

- isotope

- isotopic abundance

- relative abundance

- mass spectrum

- mass-to-charge ratio

- weighted average

- average atomic mass


Be able to interpret peaks and calculate average atomic mass:

$$\bar m=\sum(\text{isotope mass})(\text{fractional abundance})$$
### 1.3 Elemental composition of pure substances

Know:

- percent composition

- empirical formula

- molecular formula

- elemental analysis

- combustion analysis


Be able to:

$$\text{mass data} \rightarrow \text{moles of each element} \rightarrow \text{mole ratio} \rightarrow \text{empirical formula}$$
And:

$$\text{empirical formula} + \text{molar mass} \rightarrow \text{molecular formula}$$
### 1.4 Composition of mixtures

Know:

- mixture

- component

- composition

- mass fraction

- relative amount


Be able to use experimental or compositional data to determine how much of each substance is in a mixture.

### 1.5 Atomic structure and electron configuration

Know:

- nucleus

- proton

- neutron

- electron

- shell

- energy level

- subshell

- (s,p,d,f)

- core electron

- valence electron

- ground state

- electron configuration

- Aufbau principle

- Coulombic attraction

- shielding

- effective nuclear charge


Be able to write things such as:

$$\mathrm{O}:1s^22s^22p^4$$
and configurations for common ions.

Understand Coulomb's-law reasoning:

$$F\propto\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}$$
Smaller (r) or larger charge magnitude means stronger electrostatic interaction.

**You do not need to assign quantum numbers to individual electrons for AP.** College Board explicitly excludes that. ([AP中央](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-course-and-exam-description.pdf "AP® Chemistry Course and Exam Description"))

### 1.6 Photoelectron spectroscopy, PES

Know:

- photoelectron

- binding energy

- PES spectrum

- peak

- peak intensity

- subshell


Interpret:

- peak position → energy needed to remove electron

- relative peak size → number of electrons in that subshell


Be able to infer an electron configuration from a PES graph and compare two elements' spectra. ([AP中央](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-course-and-exam-description.pdf "AP® Chemistry Course and Exam Description"))

### 1.7 Periodic trends

Know and **explain**, not just memorize:

- atomic radius

- ionic radius

- ionization energy

- electronegativity

- electron shielding

- effective nuclear charge

- Coulombic attraction


Understand trends across/down the periodic table.

The important part is being able to say **why** a trend exists using nuclear charge, distance, and shielding. College Board specifically warns that students who merely memorize trends struggle with these explanations. ([AP中央](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-course-and-exam-description.pdf "AP® Chemistry Course and Exam Description"))

### 1.8 Valence electrons and ionic compounds

Know:

- valence electron

- cation

- anion

- ionic compound

- electrostatic attraction

- lattice

- formula unit

- charge neutrality


Be able to predict common ionic charges from periodic position and explain properties through electrostatic attraction.

## Topics 6.1–6.9

Official topics include endothermic/exothermic processes, energy diagrams, heat transfer, calorimetry, phase-change energy, reaction enthalpy, bond enthalpies, enthalpies of formation and Hess's law. ([AP中央](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-course-and-exam-description.pdf "AP® Chemistry Course and Exam Description"))

Know:

- system

- surroundings

- heat

- temperature

- thermal equilibrium

- endothermic

- exothermic

- heat capacity

- specific heat capacity

- calorimetry

- calorimeter

- enthalpy

- enthalpy change

- bond enthalpy

- standard enthalpy of formation

- Hess's law


Master:

$$q=mc\Delta T$$
Understand:

$$q_{\text{system}}=-q_{\text{surroundings}}$$
and:

$$\Delta H<0 \Rightarrow\text{exothermic}$$
$$\Delta H>0 \Rightarrow\text{endothermic}$$
Using bond enthalpies:

$$\Delta H \approx \sum E(\text{bonds broken}) \sum E(\text{bonds formed})$$
Using standard enthalpies of formation:

$$\Delta H^\circ_{\rm rxn} \sum n\Delta H_f^\circ(\text{products}) \sum n\Delta H_f^\circ(\text{reactants})$$
Master Hess's law, including reversing/scaling equations.

# UNIT 7: Equilibrium

## Topics 7.1–7.12

Official topics include dynamic equilibrium, reaction direction, (Q), (K), calculations, properties of (K), equilibrium concentrations, representations, Le Châtelier, solubility equilibrium and common-ion effect. ([AP中央](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-course-and-exam-description.pdf "AP® Chemistry Course and Exam Description"))

Know:

- reversible reaction

- dynamic equilibrium

- forward reaction

- reverse reaction

- equilibrium position

- equilibrium constant

- (K_c)

- (K_p)

- reaction quotient (Q)

- equilibrium expression

- ICE table

- Le Châtelier's principle

- equilibrium shift

- solubility equilibrium

- saturated solution

- (K_{sp})

- molar solubility

- common-ion effect


Critical concept:

At equilibrium:

$$\boxed{r_\text{forward}=r_\text{reverse}}$$
but:

$$[\text{reactants}] \neq [\text{products}]$$
necessarily.

For:

$$aA+bB\rightleftharpoons cC+dD$$
$$K_c= \frac{[C]^c[D]^d} {[A]^a[B]^b}$$
Know:

$$Q<K\Rightarrow\text{forward}$$
$$Q>K\Rightarrow\text{reverse}$$
$$Q=K\Rightarrow\text{equilibrium}$$
Understand what happens to (K) when:

- reaction is reversed;

- coefficients are multiplied;

- reactions are added.


And understand Le Châtelier effects of:

- concentration

- pressure/volume

- temperature


A catalyst does **not** change equilibrium position or (K).

### Core equations

$$K_w=[H_3O^+][OH^-]$$
At (25^\circ C):

$$K_w=1.0\times10^{-14}$$
$$pH=-\log[H_3O^+]$$
$$pOH=-\log[OH^-]$$
$$pH+pOH=14$$
But understand that **neutral pH is not always 7** because (K_w) changes with temperature. ([AP中央](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-course-and-exam-description.pdf "AP® Chemistry Course and Exam Description"))

$$K_a= \frac{[H_3O^+][A^-]} {[HA]}$$

$$K_b= \frac{[OH^-][HB^+]} {[B]}$$

$$K_aK_b=K_w$$

$$HA/A^-$$

$$B/HB^+$$

$$pH=pK_a+\log \frac{[A^-]}{[HA]}$$

$$[HA]=[A^-]$$
therefore:

$$\boxed{pH=pK_a}$$
([AP中央](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-course-and-exam-description.pdf "AP® Chemistry Course and Exam Description"))

([AP中央](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-course-and-exam-description.pdf "AP® Chemistry Course and Exam Description"))

### Thermodynamics vocabulary

Know:

- spontaneous

- thermodynamically favorable

- entropy

- dispersal of matter

- dispersal of energy

- standard entropy

- enthalpy

- Gibbs free energy

- standard Gibbs free energy

- thermodynamic control

- kinetic control

- coupled reaction


General tendencies:

$$\text{solid}\rightarrow\text{liquid}\rightarrow\text{gas}$$
usually means:

$$\Delta S>0$$
More gas particles generally means greater entropy. College Board emphasizes entropy as dispersal of matter and energy. ([AP中央](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-course-and-exam-description.pdf "AP® Chemistry Course and Exam Description"))

Calculate:

$$\Delta S^\circ_{\rm rxn} \sum S^\circ_{\rm products} \sum S^\circ_{\rm reactants}$$

$$\boxed{\Delta G=\Delta H-T\Delta S}$$
Interpret:

$$\Delta G<0$$
thermodynamically favorable.

$$\Delta G>0$$
thermodynamically unfavorable.

$$\Delta G=0$$
equilibrium.

And:

$$\Delta G^\circ=-RT\ln K$$
This is the bridge connecting thermodynamics and equilibrium.

- standard cell potential

$$\text{spontaneous chemical reaction} \rightarrow \text{electrical energy}$$

$$\text{electrical energy} \rightarrow \text{nonspontaneous chemical reaction}$$

$$\Delta G^\circ=-nFE^\circ$$
and the Nernst relationship provided on the reference sheet.

$$q=It$$

# The six AP Chemistry science practices

This is the part students often ignore and then wonder why knowing every equation did not magically produce a 5.

College Board explicitly assesses six science practices. ([AP中央](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-course-and-exam-description.pdf "AP® Chemistry Course and Exam Description"))

### 1. Models and Representations

You must interpret:

- particle diagrams

- Lewis structures

- PES spectra

- energy diagrams

- molecular models

- graphs

- electrochemical-cell diagrams


### 2. Question and Method

You must be able to:

- form a testable question

- formulate a hypothesis

- predict results

- identify independent/dependent variables

- choose experimental procedures

- identify experimental errors

- predict effects of procedural changes


### 3. Representing Data and Phenomena

You must:

- graph data correctly

- choose axes

- include units

- draw best-fit relationships

- create particle-level diagrams

- move between microscopic and macroscopic representations


### 4. Model Analysis

You must:

- interpret models

- determine whether a model agrees with chemical theory

- use particle behavior to explain observable properties

- identify limitations of models


### 5. Mathematical Routines

You must independently choose the correct mathematical pathway.

Not:

> “This chapter uses equation X.”

Instead:

> “I have these quantities; this relationship connects them.”

That includes significant figures and dimensional analysis.

### 6. Argumentation

AP loves **claim + evidence + reasoning**.

You need to write:

**Claim:** what happens.

**Evidence:** relevant experimental data/model/calculation.

**Reasoning:** chemical principle explaining why the evidence supports the claim.

This is often the difference between an answer that feels correct and one that actually earns the FRQ point.

- precision

- accuracy

- random error

- systematic error

- percent error

- uncertainty

- significant figures


And you must predict **direction of error**.

For example, not merely:

> “This causes error.”

AP wants reasoning like:

> “The measured concentration will be too low because…”

That last clause is where the point tends to live.

# Equations: what to memorize versus understand

The pleasant surprise is that College Board provides an equation/reference sheet on the exam. It includes equations for atomic structure, gases, solutions, kinetics, equilibrium, acids/bases, thermodynamics and electrochemistry. ([AP中央](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-equations-sheet.pdf "AP® Chemistry Exam Reference Information"))

So you don't need to waste brain storage memorizing every equation character-for-character.

You **do** need to instantly know:

- what every variable means;

- what units it requires;

- when the equation applies;

- when it does **not** apply;

- how variables relate;

- how to rearrange it;

- how to combine it with stoichiometry.


That is far more important.

# Things in Zumdahl you do NOT need to prioritize for AP

Your Zumdahl textbook is broader than the AP curriculum.

For current AP scope, College Board explicitly excludes or de-emphasizes several things that appear in full general-chemistry textbooks:

- assigning **quantum numbers** to electrons;

- (d)-orbital hybridization;

- explicit **molecular-orbital diagrams/filling**;

- detailed bonding/nonbonding/antibonding MO distinctions;

- **colligative properties**;

- solution **molality** calculations;

- solution percent-by-mass and percent-by-volume calculations;

- numerical calculations using the **Arrhenius equation**;

- giant memorized solubility-rule tables beyond the specified AP patterns. ([AP中央](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-course-and-exam-description.pdf "AP® Chemistry Course and Exam Description"))


That distinction matters. Zumdahl is an excellent source for **learning chemistry**, but the College Board CED should decide **what you need to master for AP**.

# The ultimate A*/5 mastery standard

For **every topic above**, don't stop at “I recognize this word.”

You should reach four levels:

**1. Define it**

> What is equilibrium?

**2. Explain it microscopically**

> What are the particles doing at equilibrium?

**3. Calculate with it**

$$Q,\ K,\ [A]_{\rm eq}$$
**4. Apply it to an unfamiliar situation**

> Given a new graph, experiment, particle diagram or reaction, predict what happens and justify why.

The current AP framework explicitly emphasizes moving among **macroscopic, microscopic/submicroscopic and symbolic representations**, analyzing data, designing experiments, solving mathematically and justifying claims. ([AP中央](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-course-and-exam-description.pdf "AP® Chemistry Course and Exam Description"))

If you can genuinely do those four things for **Units 1–9 plus the six science practices**, you're no longer merely “covering AP Chemistry.” You're operating at the level needed to target the top of the course and a **5 on the AP exam**.
