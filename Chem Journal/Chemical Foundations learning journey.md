## Chapter 1 — Important knowledge points

Source: Zumdahl, *Chemistry*, 9th Edition, Chapter 1, “Chemical Foundations.”

### 1.1 Chemistry: An Overview

- Chemistry studies matter, its properties, and the changes it undergoes.
- A macroscopic observation is what we can observe or measure directly.
- A microscopic explanation describes particles such as atoms and molecules.
- Chemical understanding connects observations with models and explanations.

### 1.2 The Scientific Method

- A scientific investigation commonly connects observation, question, hypothesis, experiment, evidence, and conclusion.
- A hypothesis is a testable proposed explanation.
- A theory is a well-supported explanation that organizes many observations.
- A scientific law describes a consistent relationship or pattern; it does not replace a theory.
- Scientific models are useful representations and may be revised when new evidence appears.
- The law of conservation of mass states that mass is not created or destroyed in a chemical reaction.

### 1.3 Units of Measurement

- The SI system provides standard units for scientific measurements.
- Common experiment units are grams (g) for mass, milliliters (mL) for volume, and degrees Celsius (°C) for temperature.
- A derived unit is built from other units, such as density in g/mL.
- Metric prefixes change the size of a unit: kilo-, centi-, milli-, micro-, and nano-.
- Volume relationships used in the laboratory include:

$$1\ \mathrm{mL} = 1\ \mathrm{cm^3}$$

### 1.4 Uncertainty in Measurement

- Every measured value has some uncertainty because instruments have limited resolution.
- Record all certain digits plus one estimated digit when the instrument allows it.
- Accuracy describes closeness to the accepted value.
- Precision describes how closely repeated measurements agree with one another.
- Random error causes results to vary unpredictably; repeated trials can reveal its effect.
- Systematic error shifts results in a consistent direction and is not usually removed by repetition.

### 1.5 Significant Figures and Calculations

- Significant figures communicate the precision supported by a measurement.
- Nonzero digits are significant; leading zeros are not; captive zeros are significant.
- Exact numbers and defined conversion relationships do not limit significant figures.
- For addition and subtraction, round according to decimal places.
- For multiplication and division, round according to significant figures.
- Keep extra digits during intermediate calculations and round at the end.

### 1.6 Learning to Solve Problems Systematically

- Identify the target quantity, known values, units, and relevant relationship before calculating.
- Choose an equation or method that connects the known values to the target.
- Keep units visible throughout the calculation.
- Check whether the units, size, sign, and precision of the answer make chemical sense.

### 1.7 Dimensional Analysis

- A conversion factor is a ratio equal to one, such as \(\frac{1000\ \mathrm{mL}}{1\ \mathrm{L}}\).
- Arrange conversion factors so unwanted units cancel and the desired unit remains.
- Unit cancellation is both a calculation method and an error check.

### 1.8 Temperature

- Celsius, Fahrenheit, and Kelvin are temperature scales with different zero points and degree sizes.
- Kelvin is an absolute temperature scale; its zero point is absolute zero.
- The conversion between Celsius and Kelvin is:

$$T_{\mathrm{K}} = T_{\mathrm{°C}} + 273.15$$

- Temperature must be recorded because many physical properties, including density, depend on temperature.

### 1.9 Density

- Density is mass divided by volume:

$$D = \frac{m}{V}$$

- For a liquid sample, calculate the liquid mass by subtracting the empty-container mass from the container-plus-liquid mass.
- Common laboratory density units are g/mL and g/cm³.
- Density is a physical property that can help compare a sample with reference substances.
- A density comparison supports a conclusion but does not by itself prove chemical identity.
- The sample temperature and reference density temperature must be compatible.

### 1.10 Classification of Matter

- Matter has mass and occupies space.
- A pure substance has a fixed composition and consistent properties.
- An element contains one type of atom.
- A compound contains different elements chemically joined in a fixed ratio.
- A mixture contains substances physically combined and can have variable composition.
- A homogeneous mixture is uniform throughout; a heterogeneous mixture is not uniform throughout.
- Physical properties can be observed without changing chemical identity; chemical properties describe the ability to undergo chemical change.
- A physical change does not create a new substance; a chemical change creates one or more new substances.
- Mixtures can be separated using physical methods such as filtration, distillation, and chromatography.

## Study note — Atomic structure

- An **atom（原子）** is the basic unit of an element.
- The **atomic nucleus（原子核）** is the center of the atom.
- The nucleus contains **protons（质子）** and **neutrons（中子）**.
- **Electrons（电子）** are found around the nucleus.
- A proton has a positive charge; a neutron has no charge; an electron has a negative charge.
- The number of protons determines which element the atom is.

```text
atom = nucleus + electrons
nucleus = protons + neutrons
```

**Language note:** “proton” is **质子**. “正子” usually means **positron（正电子）**, which is not a normal component of an atom.
