# Chemical Foundations — Chapter 1 Key Knowledge Points

**Source:** Steven S. Zumdahl and Susan A. Zumdahl, *Chemistry*, 9th Edition, Chapter 1.

This page collects the core ideas to understand before doing the Liquid Type Test Experiment.

## 1.1 Chemistry and matter

- **Chemistry** studies matter, its properties, and the changes it undergoes.
- **Matter** has mass and occupies space.
- A **macroscopic observation** is something we can observe or measure directly.
- A **microscopic explanation** describes particles such as atoms and molecules.
- Chemistry connects observations with models and explanations.

## 1.2 The scientific method

- A scientific investigation connects observations, questions, hypotheses, experiments, evidence, and conclusions.
- A **hypothesis** is a testable proposed explanation.
- A **theory** is a well-supported explanation that organizes many observations.
- A **scientific law** describes a consistent relationship or pattern.
- A **model** is a useful representation of a system; it may be revised when new evidence appears.
- The **law of conservation of mass** states that mass is not created or destroyed in a chemical reaction.

## 1.3 Units of measurement

- The **SI system** provides standard units for scientific measurements.
- Common laboratory units are grams (g) for mass, milliliters (mL) for volume, and degrees Celsius (°C) for temperature.
- A **derived unit** is built from other units; density can be expressed in g/mL.
- Metric prefixes include kilo-, centi-, milli-, micro-, and nano-.
- Important laboratory volume relationship:

$$1\ \mathrm{mL} = 1\ \mathrm{cm^3}$$

## 1.4 Uncertainty in measurement

- Every measurement has some **uncertainty** because instruments have limited resolution.
- Record all certain digits plus one estimated digit when appropriate.
- **Accuracy** means closeness to an accepted value.
- **Precision** means how closely repeated measurements agree with one another.
- **Random error** causes unpredictable variation; repeated trials help show its effect.
- **Systematic error** shifts results consistently and is not normally removed by repetition.

## 1.5 Significant figures and calculations

- **Significant figures** communicate the precision supported by a measurement.
- Nonzero digits are significant; leading zeros are not; captive zeros are significant.
- Exact numbers and defined conversion relationships do not limit significant figures.
- Addition and subtraction follow the decimal-place rule.
- Multiplication and division follow the significant-figure rule.
- Keep extra digits during intermediate calculations and round at the end.

## 1.6 Systematic problem solving

For every calculation:

1. Identify the target quantity.
2. List the known values with their units.
3. Choose the equation or relationship.
4. Calculate while keeping units visible.
5. Check the units, size, sign, and precision of the answer.

## 1.7 Dimensional analysis

- A **conversion factor** is a ratio equal to one, such as $\frac{1000\ \mathrm{mL}}{1\ \mathrm{L}}$.
- Arrange conversion factors so unwanted units cancel and the desired unit remains.
- Unit cancellation is both a calculation method and an error check.

## 1.8 Temperature

- Celsius, Fahrenheit, and Kelvin are different temperature scales.
- Kelvin is an absolute temperature scale; its zero point is **absolute zero**.
- Celsius-to-Kelvin conversion:

$$T_{\mathrm{K}} = T_{\mathrm{°C}} + 273.15$$

- Temperature matters because physical properties, including density, can change with temperature.

## 1.9 Density

- Density is mass divided by volume:

$$D = \frac{m}{V}$$

- For a liquid sample:

$$m_{\text{liquid}} = m_{\text{container+liquid}} - m_{\text{empty container}}$$

- Common density units are g/mL and g/cm³.
- Density is a physical property that can help compare a sample with reference substances.
- Density evidence supports a conclusion but does not, by itself, prove chemical identity.
- The sample temperature and reference-density temperature must be compatible.

## 1.10 Classification of matter

- A **pure substance** has a fixed composition and consistent properties.
- An **element** contains one type of atom.
- A **compound** contains different elements chemically joined in a fixed ratio.
- A **mixture** contains substances physically combined and can have variable composition.
- A **homogeneous mixture** is uniform throughout; a **heterogeneous mixture** is not uniform throughout.
- A **physical property** can be observed without changing chemical identity.
- A **chemical property** describes the ability to undergo chemical change.
- A **physical change** does not create a new substance.
- A **chemical change** creates one or more new substances.
- Mixtures can be separated by physical methods such as filtration, distillation, and chromatography.

## Connection to the Liquid Type Test Experiment

The experiment applies this chapter by measuring mass, volume, and temperature; calculating density; repeating trials; evaluating uncertainty, accuracy, and precision; and comparing the result with reference values. The correct conclusion is **“consistent with”** or **“inconclusive,”** not proof of identity.

## Minimum mastery check

I should be able to:

- explain accuracy versus precision;
- use units and dimensional analysis correctly;
- apply significant-figure rules;
- calculate liquid mass and density;
- explain why temperature matters;
- distinguish an element, compound, pure substance, and mixture;
- explain why density supports but does not prove identification.
